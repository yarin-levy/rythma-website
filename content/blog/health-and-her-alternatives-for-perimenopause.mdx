*Health & Her is a free hormone-health app pairing a daily symptom log with a wellness toolkit — pelvic floor training, meditation audio, interactive CBT, hydration reminders — plus a symptom checker the company says is based on the British Menopause Society's most common symptoms, and a personal task list; per its makers it is certified by the digital-health assessor ORCHA. It also sits inside a supplement business, so product recommendations are part of the experience. Women look elsewhere for four main reasons: they want their hard days predicted rather than managed after the fact, they want deeper clinician-authored education, they want peer community, or they want a tracker with no commercial product layer. The strongest alternatives in 2026 are Rythma (our app, prediction-first), Balance (education), Caria (AI guidance), Perry (community), Clue (privacy), and Flo (scale and assessment).*

Before comparing anything, it is worth saying what Health & Her does well, because for a lot of people the right move is to stay.

Its toolkit is genuinely practical. Most menopause apps tell you what is happening; Health & Her also gives you things to do about it, structured as daily habits with reminders. If your frustration is knowing roughly what would help and never actually doing it, that scaffolding is the product working as intended. The app is free, and its ORCHA certification is a reasonable credibility signal. See the [Health & Her app page](https://healthandher.com/en-us/pages/menopause-perimenopause-app) for current features.

The case for an alternative is about fit, not failure.

## Why people look for a Health & Her alternative

Perimenopause is defined by unpredictability. According to the U.S. Office on Women's Health, the transition usually starts in the mid- to late 40s and lasts about four years on average, sometimes as long as eight. Periods may run longer or shorter, skip months, and swing between unusually long and short cycles, partly because you no longer ovulate every month.

The symptom load is wide, too. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue topped the list at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all outranking hot flashes at 71%.

Against that, four gaps send people looking:

**They want forecasting, not just habits.** A hydration reminder does not help you decide whether to schedule a difficult meeting on Thursday.

**They want deeper clinical education.** The toolkit is practical rather than encyclopaedic.

**They want other women, not exercises.** Isolation is a large part of this stage, and no CBT module addresses it.

**They want no product layer.** Recommendations to supplements are disclosed and optional, but some people prefer a tool with no commercial adjacency at all.

## Rythma — prediction-first and built for irregularity

Rythma is our app, so specifics rather than praise. It is an iPhone app built for perimenopause from the start: rather than assuming a fixed monthly cycle, it expects irregularity and learns your individual symptom patterns over time.

Its distinguishing feature is prediction of difficult days. As you log, Rythma identifies your pattern and forecasts high-symptom stretches — fatigue, poor sleep, mood shifts, hot flashes, brain fog — before they arrive, so you can plan around them. It also generates a shareable doctor report summarizing symptoms and trends, which matters in appointments where midlife symptoms are easy to dismiss.

## Balance — the deepest clinician-backed education

Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, is one of the most established menopause apps: symptom tracking across midlife complaints, a large library of evidence-based articles, and a Health Report for appointments. According to its makers, Balance has been recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA.

If what you wanted from Health & Her was authoritative information and you found the toolkit thinner than expected, Balance is the direct upgrade on that axis.

**Best for:** clinician-backed education alongside tracking and a report. **Trade-offs:** broad menopause focus rather than perimenopause-specific prediction; some features are paid. See the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

## Caria — conversational AI guidance

Caria is a perimenopause and menopause companion app built for midlife, pairing symptom logging with AI-assisted guidance so you can ask questions in conversation rather than searching articles. For "is this normal, what should I ask my doctor" at the moment the question occurs, that is a real convenience — with the caveat that AI guidance is information, not medical advice.

**Best for:** on-demand answers alongside tracking. **Trade-off:** guidance-first rather than prediction-first; verify current features on its store listing.

## Perry — peer community

Perry is a perimenopause app built around community alongside tracking. Its value is other women in the same stage, which addresses something no exercise module can: the specific isolation of having symptoms that are common but unrecognized. Given that fatigue, irritability, and low mood outrank hot flashes in reported frequency yet are rarely attributed to perimenopause, hearing "yes, me too" is its own correction.

**Best for:** shared experience and validation. **Trade-off:** community-first rather than prediction-first; check its listing for current tracking features.

## Clue — privacy-forward tracking

Clue is a long-running, science-forward period and cycle tracker headquartered in Berlin and, per its own policy, handling sensitive health data under strict EU data-protection rules. Its perimenopause mode adds symptom tracking and, per its makers, a cycle view that accounts for changing cycle lengths.

If your reason for leaving is that you want a tool with no commercial product layer and a strong data record, Clue is the natural comparison.

**Best for:** privacy-conscious users wanting a calm, science-forward tracker. **Trade-off:** cycle-first by origin; perimenopause is one mode among several. See the [Clue perimenopause overview](https://helloclue.com/articles/menopause/introducing-clue-perimenopause).

## Flo — scale, assessment, and community

Flo is the largest general period and cycle tracker with a dedicated perimenopause experience. According to Flo, that includes a Perimenopause Score the company describes as the first digital assessment tool designed and validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms, symptom tracking, a window-based next-period estimate rather than a single date, doctor-question guidance, and an anonymous in-app community.

**Best for:** a polished tracker with a structured assessment and a community. **Trade-off:** cycle-first origin; perimenopause is an added experience. See Flo's [perimenopause announcement](https://flo.health/newsroom/flo-for-perimenopause-is-launching-to-empower-the-1-billion-women-who-experience-perimenopause-without-the-support-they-deserve).

## How to choose a Health & Her alternative

A quick way through it: for **anticipating hard days and a doctor report**, a prediction-first app like Rythma; for **the deepest education**, Balance; for **AI answers on demand**, Caria; for **peer community**, Perry; for **privacy and no product layer**, Clue; for **scale, assessment, and community**, Flo. And if the daily toolkit is what keeps you consistent, staying with Health & Her is perfectly reasonable.

Most of these are free to download, so trying one or two costs little. Whatever you choose, the value comes from consistent logging — patterns only emerge once there is enough data behind them.

## When to stop consulting an app

No tracker replaces medical assessment. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause.
