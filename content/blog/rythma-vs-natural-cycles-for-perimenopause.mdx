## Natural Cycles — a regulated contraceptive method

Natural Cycles occupies a genuinely distinct position in this category. It is not a general wellness tracker: it is a regulated medical product, the first birth-control app cleared by the FDA, in 2018.

Its method uses daily basal body temperature readings plus cycle data to identify fertile and non-fertile days, which the app presents for contraception or for conception depending on the mode you use. That regulatory clearance is a meaningful distinction — it is a different category of claim from a symptom tracker's, and it is verifiable rather than marketing. You can read its method and regulatory details on the [Natural Cycles science page](https://www.naturalcycles.com/the-science).

**Best for:** people who specifically want a hormone-free, FDA-cleared contraceptive method and are willing to take a daily temperature and follow the method precisely.

**Trade-offs:** it is built around ovulatory cycles. It is not designed to interpret midlife symptoms, and it does not set out to track or explain perimenopause.

## Why perimenopause complicates a temperature-based method

This is the part that matters, and it is a clinical point rather than a product one.

Fertility-awareness methods work by detecting the hormonal signature of ovulation — in this case the small sustained temperature rise that follows it. Perimenopause disrupts exactly that. According to the U.S. Office on Women's Health, during the transition periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and **you may not ovulate every cycle**. The Cleveland Clinic describes hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster" rather than declining smoothly.

Anovulatory cycles are also concentrated in the later part of the transition. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, and the late transition by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period.

Two things follow. Interpreting temperature data gets harder when ovulation is inconsistent. And — importantly — pregnancy remains possible during perimenopause. Irregular cycles do not mean infertile cycles, and the fact that periods have become unpredictable is not on its own a reason to stop using contraception.

That combination is why contraception in your 40s is a question for a healthcare professional rather than an app comparison. Ask specifically about what is appropriate for your stage and how long to continue it.

## Rythma — built for the symptom side of the same decade

Rythma is our app, so specifics rather than praise. It is an iPhone app built for perimenopause from the start: instead of assuming a fixed monthly cycle, it expects irregularity and learns your individual symptom patterns over time.

Its purpose is prediction of difficult days. As you log, Rythma identifies your pattern and forecasts high-symptom stretches — fatigue, poor sleep, mood shifts, hot flashes, brain fog — before they arrive, so you can plan around them. It also generates a shareable doctor report summarizing symptoms and trends.

To be explicit about scope: **Rythma is not a contraceptive app and makes no fertility claims.** It is a tracking and educational tool, not a medical device. If you need contraception, that is a separate decision made with a clinician.

**Best for:** women whose cycles have become irregular and who want to anticipate hard days and bring evidence to an appointment.

**Trade-offs:** iPhone-only today; prediction improves the longer you log; and it does nothing for family planning. See the [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rythma-perimenopause-tracker/id6762185611).

## Side by side

**Different jobs.** Natural Cycles answers "which days am I fertile." Rythma answers "which days are likely to be hard." Neither answers the other's question, and no app answers both well.

**Different regulatory status.** Natural Cycles is a cleared contraceptive product. Rythma is a tracking and educational tool, not a medical device. That distinction should inform how much weight you put on each app's output.

**Different relationship to irregularity.** Natural Cycles depends on ovulatory regularity and gets harder to use as that degrades. Rythma is designed on the assumption that regularity has already gone.

**The doctor visit.** Rythma generates a symptom report. Either way, tracking is how you catch the changes the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says warrant medical attention — very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon hourly for two or more hours), bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles staying consistently closer than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause.

## What to actually do

- **You need contraception and you are in your 40s.** Talk to a clinician about what is appropriate now and how long to continue it. Do not treat irregular periods as protection.
- **You want to understand and plan around your symptoms.** That is what a perimenopause app is for. Rythma is built around forecasting; Balance leads on education; Caria offers AI guidance; Perry offers community.
- **You want both.** They are separate tools and separate conversations. Using a symptom tracker does not replace a contraception decision, and using a fertility app does not help you plan around a bad week.
