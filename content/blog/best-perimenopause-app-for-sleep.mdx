*The best app for perimenopause sleep is one that records waking alongside night sweats, mood, and the next day's symptoms — because in midlife the useful question is rarely "how many hours did I get" but "what woke me, and what did it cost me tomorrow." Sleep problems affect 76% of women in this stage according to an international survey of more than 17,000 women analyzed by The Menopause Society, ranking fourth behind fatigue (83%), irritability (80%), and depressive mood (77%). Dedicated sleep trackers measure duration and stages well but know nothing about hormones. Perimenopause apps understand the context but rarely measure sleep directly. In 2026 the practical answer is usually a pairing: passive sleep data from Apple Health or a wearable, plus a perimenopause app that interprets it — Rythma (our app) for forecasting hard days, Balance for education, Health & Her for sleep-focused wellness tools, Clue or Flo for cycle-aware logging.*

If you are awake at 3am reading this, the thing you want is not a sleep score. You want to know why this keeps happening and whether tomorrow is going to be a write-off.

We disclose that Rythma is our own app, and describe every other tool from its own published materials.

## Why perimenopause sleep needs a different kind of tracking

**The waking is often symptom-driven, not sleep-disorder-driven.** Night sweats are the classic culprit: you wake hot, damp, and alert, and the cause is a thermoregulatory event rather than a sleep-architecture problem. A tracker that reports "you woke 4 times" without capturing what woke you has recorded the effect and missed the cause.

**Hormones are not on a schedule.** The Cleveland Clinic describes perimenopausal hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone rather than declining smoothly. That means bad sleep tends to come in stretches rather than distributed evenly, which is exactly the pattern a daily score obscures and a longer view reveals.

**Sleep is the multiplier for everything else.** Fatigue, irritability, and low mood — the three most reported symptoms of this stage — all worsen on broken sleep. Improving sleep often does more for the overall picture than treating any single symptom, which is why it is worth tracking properly rather than approximately.

**The NHS lists sleep problems among common perimenopause symptoms**, alongside hot flushes, night sweats, mood changes, brain fog, weight gain, palpitations, and joint aches. This is a recognized part of the transition, not a personal failing of sleep hygiene.

## What to look for in an app

**It logs what woke you, not just that you woke.** Night sweats, needing the bathroom, anxiety, pain, noise — these lead in different directions.

**It connects night to next day.** The reason to track a bad night is to see its cost. An app that shows last night's sleep beside today's fatigue and mood is answering the question you actually have.

**It handles clustering.** Look for a view of weeks, not days. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, so any rhythm in your sleep will be loose rather than calendar-regular.

**It does not moralize.** Sleep-hygiene lectures land badly when the cause is a hot flash at 3am.

## Apple Health and wearables — the measurement layer

Apple Health, and the Apple Watch or another wearable, is where actual sleep measurement happens: duration, timing, and stages, collected passively without you doing anything. On supported Apple Watch models, wrist temperature sensing is also recorded overnight.

That passivity is its strength. Nobody logs their sleep accurately at 3am, and a device that measures it while you are unconscious removes the weakest link. What it cannot do is know why you woke or what it means in the context of perimenopause.

**Best for:** objective, effortless measurement. **Trade-offs:** no hormonal or midlife interpretation.

Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, offers symptom tracking, a large library of evidence-based articles, and a Health Report for appointments. Per its makers it has been recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA.

If what you need is a clear explanation of why perimenopause disrupts sleep and what the management options are, this is the strongest option on that axis. See the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

## Clue and Flo — cycle-aware symptom logging

Clue is a science-forward tracker headquartered in Berlin that, per its own policy, handles health data under strict EU data-protection rules; its perimenopause mode adds symptom tracking and a cycle view accounting for changing cycle lengths. Flo's perimenopause experience includes, per the company, a Perimenopause Score it describes as the first digital assessment tool designed and validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms, plus tracking for sleep, hot flashes, mood, and fatigue and an anonymous community.

**Best for:** Clue if privacy leads; Flo if you want an assessment and community. **Trade-offs:** both are cycle-first by origin; neither forecasts difficult days.

## The practical answer: pair them

Most women get the best result from a wearable or Apple Health measuring sleep passively, plus one perimenopause app interpreting it in context. Measurement without interpretation gives you numbers you cannot act on; interpretation without measurement relies on how you remember a night you were barely conscious for.

Whatever you use, log the cause of waking, not just the fact of it, and give it several weeks before drawing conclusions.

## When to raise it with a doctor

Persistent sleep disruption is worth a conversation rather than endurance — particularly if night sweats are the trigger, since that is a treatable symptom rather than a sleep problem. Raise it promptly if broken sleep is accompanied by persistent low mood or hopelessness; depressive mood was reported by 77% of women in the Menopause Society survey, and it is treatable. Separately, ACOG advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles consistently closer than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause.
