## Stardust — cycle phases, wearable data, and a distinctive frame

Per its own description, Stardust is a women-built and data-encrypted period, hormone, ovulation, and pregnancy app that blends science and astronomy in tracking the menstrual cycle.

Its features include **daily symptom and bodily signal logging** — from feelings and cravings to activities and cervical mucus — with the app linking common symptoms to specific phases of your cycle. It **reads data from Apple Health and wearables** to surface sleep, energy, and activity trends across cycle phases. It frames everything through the **four cycle phases**: period, follicular, ovulation, and luteal, with information about the hormonal changes in each. It offers a **pregnancy mode**, and **partner and friend sharing** so people close to you can follow along. Its materials emphasize that your data is private.

That phase framing is the core of the experience, and it is genuinely useful for the audience it was built for. Understanding that your energy and mood follow a hormonal arc — rather than being random — is a real insight when the arc exists. See the [Stardust app features page](https://stardust.app/app-features).

**Best for:** people with recognizable cycles who want phase-aware insight, wearable integration, and a distinctive, non-clinical presentation.

**Trade-offs:** the framework depends on identifiable cycle phases; it is not built around midlife symptom interpretation.

## Why phase framing gets harder in perimenopause

This is not a criticism of Stardust so much as a description of what the transition does.

The four-phase model rests on ovulation happening. The follicular phase leads to it; the luteal phase follows it and is defined by the progesterone the corpus luteum produces afterward. Remove reliable ovulation and the phases stop being reliably identifiable.

That is precisely what perimenopause does. According to the U.S. Office on Women's Health, during the transition periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and **you may not ovulate every cycle**. The Cleveland Clinic describes hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone rather than declining smoothly.

Clinically, the instability is the definition. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition begins when consecutive cycle lengths persistently differ by seven or more days, and the late transition is marked by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period.

So a phase-based app in late perimenopause will increasingly be telling you which phase you are in based on assumptions that no longer hold — which is not the app failing so much as the input disappearing.

## Rythma — symptom-first, built for the instability

Rythma is our app, so specifics rather than praise. It is an iPhone app built for perimenopause from the start: rather than organizing your month around cycle position, it treats your symptoms as the primary data and learns your individual patterns from them.

Its distinguishing feature is prediction of difficult days. As you log, Rythma forecasts likely high-symptom stretches — fatigue, poor sleep, mood shifts, hot flashes, brain fog — before they arrive, so you can plan around them. Because that forecast is built from your symptom history rather than from cycle-length averages, it does not require ovulation to be happening on schedule.

It also generates a shareable doctor report summarizing symptoms and trends, which matters given how routinely midlife symptoms get attributed to stress.

**Best for:** women whose cycles have become unpredictable and who want to anticipate hard days and bring evidence to an appointment.

**Trade-offs:** iPhone-only today; prediction improves the longer you log, so early weeks are mostly data-gathering; no wearable-data integration, partner sharing, or pregnancy mode. See the [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rythma-perimenopause-tracker/id6762185611).

## Head-to-head

**What organizes the app.** Stardust organizes around cycle phase. Rythma organizes around symptom pattern. That single difference cascades through everything else.

**Symptom coverage.** Stardust's logging is broad and phase-linked. Rythma's is midlife-focused. The relevant question is which symptoms actually dominate your experience: in an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue led at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all above hot flashes at 71%.

**Wearable data.** Stardust reads sleep, energy, and activity from Apple Health and wearables, which is a genuine advantage for passive measurement. Rythma relies on what you log.

**Sharing.** Stardust's partner mode has no equivalent in Rythma. If involving a partner matters to you, that is a real point of difference.

**The appointment.** Rythma generates a doctor report. Either app's log helps you catch what the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says warrants attention — very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon hourly for two or more hours), bleeding between periods or after sex, or cycles staying consistently closer than about 21 days.

## How to choose

- **Your cycle still has recognizable structure and you like the phase lens.** Stardust.
- **Your cycle has become genuinely unpredictable.** A symptom-first app fits better.
- **You want passive sleep and activity data pulled in.** Stardust; or pair Apple Health with a perimenopause app.
- **You want a partner following along.** Stardust's sharing.
- **You want hard days forecast and a doctor report.** Rythma.

Both are free to download, so trying one for a few weeks costs little. Whichever you pick, log consistently — the value in either depends on there being enough data behind it.
