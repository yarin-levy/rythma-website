*Balance and Caria are both midlife-first apps, and they answer the same underlying need — "help me understand what is happening to me" — in opposite ways. Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, offers symptom tracking, a large library of evidence-based articles, and a Health Report for appointments; per its makers it has been recognized by the NHS and certified by the digital-health assessor ORCHA. Caria pairs symptom logging with conversational AI guidance, so you ask a question and get an answer framed for this stage. Choose Balance if you want depth, authorship, and clinical credibility you can point to. Choose Caria if you want an immediate answer at the moment the question occurs. Neither is built to forecast which of your days will be difficult.*

This is a comparison between two apps we do not make. We do make a perimenopause app — Rythma — and we say so where it is relevant at the end, but the bulk of this is a straight assessment of Balance and Caria, written from each company's own published materials.

## The need both are answering

Perimenopause arrives with very little preparation. It usually begins in the mid- to late 40s and lasts about four years on average, sometimes as long as eight, according to the U.S. Office on Women's Health. Periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and you may not ovulate every cycle. The Cleveland Clinic describes hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster" rather than declining smoothly.

Meanwhile the symptom set is far wider than most people expect. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue was the most reported symptom at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all outranking hot flashes at 71%.

The result is a stage where the dominant experience is confusion, and where the most common early request is not a feature but an explanation. Balance and Caria both take that seriously.

## Balance — depth, authorship, and clinical credibility

Balance is among the most established menopause apps. Alongside symptom tracking across midlife complaints, its defining asset is a large library of evidence-based articles, and it generates a Health Report you can take to appointments. According to its makers, Balance has been recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA.

What Balance gives you that a chat interface cannot is **provenance**. The content has authors, an editorial position, and an organization standing behind it. In a health domain where the surrounding internet is saturated with confident nonsense and supplement marketing, being able to point at where an explanation came from has real value — including when you want to raise something with a clinician who may be sceptical.

Its Health Report is also a practical asset. Ten-minute appointments do not favour recall, and arriving with a structured summary changes the conversation.

**Best for:** women who want authoritative, clinician-backed education alongside tracking and a report for appointments.

**Trade-offs:** the focus is broad menopause rather than perimenopause-specific prediction, some features sit behind a paid tier, and reading is a slower path to an answer than asking. See the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

## Caria — an answer at the moment you have the question

Caria is a perimenopause and menopause companion app built for midlife rather than adapted from a reproductive-age tracker. Alongside symptom logging, its distinguishing feature is AI-assisted guidance: you ask in plain language and get information framed for this stage.

The advantage is immediacy and specificity. "Is it normal to have two periods in three weeks and then nothing for two months" is a question a library answers indirectly and a conversation answers directly. At 2am, when a great deal of perimenopause thinking happens, that difference is not trivial.

The trade-off is provenance. AI-generated guidance is information rather than medical advice, and it does not carry the same citable authorship as an article with a named clinician behind it. Treat it as a fast orientation tool rather than a source you would quote to a doctor.

**Best for:** women who want conversational, always-available answers alongside tracking.

**Trade-offs:** guidance-first rather than prediction-first; AI answers are not clinical advice; verify current features on its store listing.

## Head-to-head

**Depth vs. speed.** Balance rewards reading; Caria rewards asking. Which suits you is mostly a question of how you prefer to learn, and both preferences are legitimate.

**Credibility you can cite.** Balance wins clearly here. If part of your problem is being dismissed and you want something with an organization behind it, that matters.

**Personalization.** Caria's conversational format naturally handles your specific situation; a library requires you to find the article that matches it.

**The appointment.** Balance generates a Health Report designed for it. With Caria you have your log and whatever you noted. Either way, tracking is what lets you spot the bleeding changes the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says warrant medical attention — very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon hourly for two or more hours), bleeding between periods or after sex, or cycles staying consistently closer than about 21 days.

**Irregularity.** Neither app assumes a regular cycle, which is why both suit this stage far better than a standard period tracker. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, and the late transition by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period.

**What neither does.** Neither Balance nor Caria is built to learn your individual symptom pattern and forecast which specific days ahead are likely to be difficult. Explanation and answers are both retrospective; planning requires a forecast. That is a third job, and it is the one our own app, Rythma, is built around — worth knowing about if what you actually want is to move a meeting off a bad day rather than understand why the day was bad.

## How to choose

- **You want to understand this properly and cite what you read.** Balance.
- **You want a fast answer to a specific question right now.** Caria.
- **You want a report for an appointment.** Balance generates one.
- **You want to know which days will be hard so you can plan.** Neither — look at prediction-first apps.

Both are free to download with paid tiers, so trying either costs little. Whichever you use, log consistently: any pattern you eventually see depends on there being enough data behind it.
