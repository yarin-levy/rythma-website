*Almost every perimenopause app records; very few forecast. The distinction matters because recording answers "what happened" and forecasting answers "what should I protect next week" — and only the second changes what you can do. Prediction in this stage cannot mean predicting your period: that depends on cycle-length stability, and the STRAW+10 staging system defines the early menopause transition by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days. What can be forecast is symptom load, because that is built from your own symptom history rather than from a calendar. Rythma (our app) is built around exactly that. Flo offers a Perimenopause Score the company describes as validated for perimenopause symptoms — an assessment of now rather than a forecast. Apple Health flags cycle deviations from six months of history. Balance, Caria, Perry, Clue and Health & Her explain, answer, support, or log rather than predict.*

"Predicts your symptoms" is a phrase that deserves scrutiny, including when we use it about our own product. This is an attempt to be precise about what is and is not possible.

We disclose that Rythma is our app. Everything about the others comes from their own published materials.

## What cannot honestly be predicted

**Your next period date, in late perimenopause.** Standard prediction averages recent cycle lengths and projects forward. According to the U.S. Office on Women's Health, during the transition periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and you may not ovulate every cycle. Under STRAW+10, the late transition is marked by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period. There is no method that recovers a reliable date from that. Treat confident date predictions with suspicion.

**Which stage you are in, from an app.** Staging is clinical. An app can show you patterns consistent with a stage; it cannot diagnose one.

**Individual events.** No app can tell you a hot flash will occur at 3:15pm. Symptom load across a stretch of days is a different and more tractable question than a single event.

**Anything, from thin data.** Every prediction claim in this category depends on consistent logging over weeks. An app that produces confident forecasts from ten days of scattered entries is producing decoration.

## What can be forecast, and why

Symptom load can be, at least partially, because it does not depend on the thing perimenopause destroys.

Cycle prediction needs cycle-length stability. Symptom-load forecasting needs **your own symptom history** — how your fatigue, sleep, mood, and flashes have behaved together in the past, and what tends to precede a bad stretch. That input survives irregularity.

It works because perimenopause symptoms cluster rather than distributing evenly. The Cleveland Clinic describes hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone rather than declining smoothly. Swings produce runs of difficult days. Runs have leading edges. Leading edges can be recognized.

The honest framing is probabilistic: not "Tuesday will be bad" but "the next few days look higher-risk than usual, given what your last few days looked like." That is still enough to move a meeting.

## Flo — assessment rather than forecast

Flo's perimenopause experience includes, per the company, a Perimenopause Score it describes as the first digital assessment tool designed and validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms, plus symptom tracking, a window-based next-period estimate rather than a single date, doctor-question guidance, and an anonymous community.

The score is an assessment of where you are now, not a forecast of where you will be next week — a genuinely useful thing, but a different one. The prediction window is also worth crediting as an honest retreat from false precision.

**Best for:** a structured read on your current symptom burden plus community. **Trade-offs:** cycle-first origin; assessment rather than forecast.

## Apple Health — detection rather than prediction

Apple's Cycle Tracking is free on every iPhone. Per Apple, its Cycle Deviation Detection can notify you if your logged history from the previous six months shows a pattern of irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting, and lets you export the last 12 months as a PDF for a healthcare provider.

That is detection of a change that has already happened, which is valuable — for many women it is the first concrete signal — but it is retrospective by design. See Apple's [Cycle Tracking support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356).

**Best for:** a free flag that something has shifted, plus a doctor-ready cycle history. **Trade-offs:** no symptom forecasting.

## The rest — and what they do instead

**Balance** offers clinician-backed education and a Health Report; per its makers it has been recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA. **Caria** offers conversational AI guidance for questions as they arise. **Perry** offers peer community. **Health & Her** offers a free tracker plus a wellness toolkit including CBT-style tools. **Clue** offers privacy-first logging with a perimenopause cycle view that accounts for changing cycle lengths.

None of these claims to forecast difficult days, and that is not a criticism — explanation, support, and honest logging are legitimate jobs. It is just worth knowing which job you are buying.

## How to judge a prediction claim

Ask four questions of any app promising to predict.

**Predict what, exactly?** A period date, a symptom score, or a difficult stretch? These are very different claims.

**Based on what input?** Cycle-length averages will fail here. Personal symptom history will not.

**After how long?** Any credible forecast needs weeks of consistent data. Ask how long before output is meaningful.

**How confident does it sound?** Appropriate hedging is a good sign in a domain this variable. Certainty is the warning sign.

## What prediction does not replace

Tracking is not assessment. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause. And in an international survey of more than 17,000 women analyzed by The Menopause Society, depressive mood was reported by 77% — persistent low mood is a medical conversation, not a forecasting problem.
