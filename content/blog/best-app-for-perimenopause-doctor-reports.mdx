There is a specific frustration that sends women looking for this. You have a ten-minute appointment, three months of symptoms, and the knowledge that midlife complaints get attributed to stress with depressing regularity. Reconstructing it all in the room does not work, and you know it.

A report solves a real problem. We disclose that Rythma is our own app and describe the others from their own published materials.

## Why documentation changes the appointment

**Recall is genuinely poor for fluctuating symptoms.** Not through carelessness — you cannot accurately recall how many bad nights you had in March. You remember the worst one and roughly how you felt overall.

**The symptom set is wide and easy to fragment.** In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue was the most reported symptom at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all outranking hot flashes at 71%. The NHS adds brain fog, weight gain, palpitations, and joint aches. Presented one at a time, these look like unrelated complaints. Presented together, they look like a stage.

**Bleeding patterns are clinically important and hard to describe.** ACOG advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause. "It's been heavy sometimes" does not convey that; a dated record does.

**A document shifts the burden of proof.** This is the part women mention most. Arriving with data changes how the conversation starts.

## Balance — a Health Report backed by a clinical brand

Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, offers symptom tracking across midlife complaints, a large evidence-based article library, and a Health Report you can take to appointments. According to its makers, Balance has been recognized by the NHS and certified by the digital-health assessor ORCHA.

Its particular strength here is credibility by association: a report from a clinician-founded, NHS-recognized product carries a certain weight, and the article library helps you frame what you are asking for rather than only what you are experiencing.

**Best for:** women who want a report plus the background reading to argue their case. **Trade-offs:** broad menopause focus; some features are paid. See the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

## Apple Health — free, and stronger than expected on bleeding

Apple's Cycle Tracking is built into every iPhone at no cost. Per Apple, its Cycle Deviation Detection can notify you if your logged history from the previous six months shows a pattern of irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting — and if a deviation is detected, you can export the last 12 months of cycle history as a PDF to share with your healthcare provider.

That is a genuinely strong free option for the bleeding half of the conversation, and it maps closely onto the ACOG red flags above. What it does not produce is a symptom summary: fatigue, mood, sleep, and brain fog are not what it documents. See Apple's [Cycle Tracking support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356).

**Best for:** a free, dated bleeding history for your doctor. **Trade-offs:** cycle-centred; no midlife symptom narrative.

## Flo — doctor-question framing and an assessment score

Flo's perimenopause experience includes, per the company, a Perimenopause Score it describes as the first digital assessment tool designed and validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms, symptom tracking, a window-based next-period estimate, guidance framed around questions to raise with your doctor, and an anonymous community.

Its contribution is different from a report: it helps you know what to ask. For a first appointment where you are not sure what you are even requesting, that has real value. See Flo's [perimenopause announcement](https://flo.health/newsroom/flo-for-perimenopause-is-launching-to-empower-the-1-billion-women-who-experience-perimenopause-without-the-support-they-deserve).

**Best for:** preparing questions and having an assessment result to reference. **Trade-offs:** cycle-first origin.

## Choosing by what your appointment is about

- **"My bleeding has changed and I am worried."** Apple Health's PDF export, free, covers this well.
- **"I feel terrible in a dozen ways and nobody is joining them up."** A symptom-led report — Rythma's or Balance's.
- **"I want to discuss treatment options and be taken seriously."** Balance for the report plus the reading.
- **"I do not know what to ask for."** Flo's doctor-question framing.

Using two is entirely reasonable — a cycle history from Apple Health alongside a symptom report from a perimenopause app covers both halves.

## Making the report actually work

**Log for at least eight weeks before the appointment.** A fortnight of data documents a fortnight.

**Log intensity, not just occurrence.** "Hot flashes: yes" tells a clinician nothing. Severity and frequency are what get assessed.

**Do not stop logging bleeding** even when it becomes erratic, because erratic is the clinically interesting part.

**Bring one page and one clear ask.** A report helps; forty pages of raw logs does not. Know the two or three things you most want addressed and lead with them.

**Print it or have it open before you go in.** Fumbling for an app export eats the appointment you were trying to make count.
