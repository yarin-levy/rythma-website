## What changes in perimenopause, and why it matters for both apps

Perimenopause is the transition before your final period. The U.S. Office on Women's Health puts its typical onset in the mid- to late 40s, lasting about four years on average and sometimes as long as eight. During it, periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and you may not ovulate every cycle.

That last point is the one that breaks cycle prediction. A standard tracker forecasts your next period by averaging recent cycle lengths — a method that works only while those lengths are stable. Under the STRAW+10 staging system used by clinicians, the early menopause transition is literally defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, and the late transition by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period. The input the algorithm depends on is exactly what disappears.

The symptom set widens too. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue was the most reported symptom at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all outranking hot flashes at 71%. So the useful question is not just "which app predicts my period better" but "which one helps with everything else."

## Flo — scale, an assessment score, and community

Flo is the largest general period and cycle tracker, and it has invested in a dedicated perimenopause experience.

According to Flo, that experience includes a Perimenopause Score, which the company describes as the first digital assessment tool designed and validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms; tracking for hot flashes, mood changes, fatigue, and sleep; a window-based next-period prediction rather than a single date; guidance framed around questions to raise with your doctor; and an anonymous in-app community.

Two of those are genuinely thoughtful responses to irregularity. Shifting from a single predicted date to a window is an honest acknowledgment that precision is no longer available. And an assessment score gives you something concrete to bring to an appointment, which is more than most trackers offer.

The community is also a real draw. Perimenopause is isolating partly because its most common symptoms — fatigue, irritability, low mood — are the least recognized as belonging to it. Reading other people describe the same thing has value that no algorithm replaces.

**Best for:** women who want a large, polished, well-resourced tracker with a structured perimenopause assessment and a built-in community.

**Trade-offs:** cycle-first by origin, so perimenopause is an added experience rather than the foundation; some features sit behind a paid tier. See Flo's [perimenopause announcement](https://flo.health/newsroom/flo-for-perimenopause-is-launching-to-empower-the-1-billion-women-who-experience-perimenopause-without-the-support-they-deserve).

## Clue — privacy, science-forward framing, and an adaptive cycle view

Clue is a long-running period and cycle tracker headquartered in Berlin. Per its own policy, it handles sensitive health data under strict EU data-protection rules — a reason many users specifically choose it, and a genuine differentiator in a category where data handling is a live concern.

Clue Perimenopause adds symptom tracking for this stage and, per its makers, a cycle view designed to account for changing cycle lengths rather than simply flagging a period as late. That is a meaningful design choice: an app that tells you that you are "12 days late" every month is not describing perimenopause, it is misdescribing it.

Clue's overall feel is science-forward and restrained — less gamified than most of the category, with an emphasis on what the data does and does not show. If that tone suits you, it is a plus in its own right.

**Best for:** anyone who puts privacy and a science-forward, low-hype approach at the top of the list, and wants a trusted tracker that now flexes for irregularity.

**Trade-offs:** cycle-first by origin, with perimenopause as one mode among several; no community equivalent to Flo's. See the [Clue perimenopause overview](https://helloclue.com/articles/menopause/introducing-clue-perimenopause).

## Head-to-head: where they actually diverge

**Privacy.** This is Clue's clearest advantage. Its Berlin base and EU data-protection posture give it a reputation Flo does not match on this axis. If privacy is your top decision factor, read both current privacy policies directly and weigh Clue seriously.

**Assessment and structure.** This is Flo's clearest advantage. A validated perimenopause assessment plus doctor-question prompts gives you more scaffolding than Clue's more open-ended tracking.

**Community.** Flo has one; Clue does not. If hearing from other women matters to you, that decides it.

**Handling irregularity.** Both have responded sensibly — Flo with a prediction window, Clue with an adaptive cycle view. Neither claims to restore accurate prediction, because it is not available. Treat any app that promises a precise next-period date in late perimenopause with suspicion.

**What neither is built to do.** Both are cycle trackers with perimenopause modes. Neither is designed to learn your individual symptom pattern and forecast which specific days are likely to be difficult, which is a different problem from estimating a period date. If that is what you are after, you are looking for a prediction-first, perimenopause-native app — the category our own app, Rythma, sits in, alongside options like Balance, Caria, and Perry.

## How to choose between them

- **Privacy is your top priority.** Clue.
- **You want a structured assessment and doctor-question prompts.** Flo.
- **You want a community of women in the same stage.** Flo.
- **You want a calm, science-forward tracker with no hype.** Clue.
- **You want to know which days will be hard, not when your period is due.** Neither is built for that — look at perimenopause-native apps instead.

Both are free to download, so trying one for a cycle or two costs little. Whichever you land on, log consistently: whatever intelligence an app offers only appears once there is enough data behind it.

## When to stop tracking and call a doctor

No app replaces medical assessment. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause. Tracking flow and timing is precisely what makes those patterns easy to spot and describe.
