*Mood is the perimenopause symptom most worth tracking and the one you can least assess from memory. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, irritability was reported by 80% and depressive mood by 77% — both ranking above hot flashes at 71%, and behind only fatigue at 83%. A good mood tracker for this stage does three things a generic mood app does not: it records mood beside sleep and hot flashes so you can see what drives what; it shows stretches rather than days, since hormones fluctuate rather than following a calendar; and it does not tie your mood to a cycle phase that may no longer exist. In 2026: Rythma (our app) for connecting mood to forecast hard days, Balance for clinician-backed context, Health & Her for CBT-style tools, Caria for on-demand answers, Perry for peer validation, Clue or Flo for cycle-aware logging.*

If you have found yourself furious at a dishwasher, tearful at an advert, or flat in a way that does not match your life, you are experiencing the most-reported and least-discussed part of this transition.

## Why mood needs different tracking in midlife

**It is the symptom memory handles worst.** You remember the argument, not the four broken nights before it. Retrospective mood ratings are heavily coloured by how you feel while rating them, which is precisely why a daily record beats a monthly reflection.

**Its drivers are usually elsewhere.** Sleep problems affect 76% of women in this stage, and frustration tolerance collapses on broken sleep in anyone. A great deal of what gets experienced as a mood problem is a sleep problem with a mood symptom. You only see that if both are logged.

**It does not follow a calendar.** The Cleveland Clinic describes perimenopausal hormone levels going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days. Expect clusters of hard days, not a monthly pattern — and be wary of any app confident about which cycle phase explains your mood.

**Being able to show it changes appointments.** "I have been irritable" is easy to wave away. Three months of data showing irritability, poor sleep, and fatigue moving together is a different conversation.

## What to look for

A mood tracker for perimenopause should let you log **mood alongside sleep, hot flashes, energy, and bleeding** in one place, record **intensity** rather than just a label, show **multi-week views** so stretches are visible, separate **irritability, anxiety, and low mood** rather than collapsing them into one "mood" score, and avoid **explaining your mood by cycle phase** when the cycle is unreliable.

Generic mood apps do the first two well and the rest not at all, which is why a perimenopause-specific tool usually wins here despite being less polished on the mood-logging interface itself.

## Balance — understanding why mood changes

Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, offers symptom tracking, a large library of evidence-based articles, and a Health Report for appointments; per its makers it has been recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA.

Its value for mood is context. Understanding that irritability and low mood are among the most reported features of this stage — rather than personal failings — changes how you interpret your own week, and gives you language for an appointment. See the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

**Best for:** clinician-backed explanation alongside tracking. **Trade-offs:** broad menopause focus; no forecasting.

## Health & Her — CBT-style tools alongside logging

Health & Her's free app pairs a daily symptom log and symptom checker with a wellness toolkit that includes interactive cognitive behavioural therapy, meditation audio, and a personal task list with reminders; per its makers it is certified by ORCHA. Note it sits inside a supplement business and surfaces product recommendations. See the [Health & Her app page](https://healthandher.com/en-us/pages/menopause-perimenopause-app).

**Best for:** wanting structured tools to work with, not just a record. **Trade-offs:** wellness-toolkit focus rather than pattern prediction.

## Caria and Perry — answers and validation

**Caria** pairs symptom logging with conversational AI guidance, so "is it normal to feel like this" gets an immediate answer framed for this stage — information rather than medical advice, but useful at the moment the question arises.

**Perry** is built around peer community. For mood specifically this matters more than for most symptoms: the isolation of feeling unlike yourself, with symptoms nobody recognizes as belonging to perimenopause, is corrected more effectively by another woman describing the same thing than by any feature.

**Best for:** Caria for immediate answers, Perry for validation. **Trade-offs:** neither forecasts hard days.

## Clue and Flo — cycle-aware logging

**Clue** is a science-forward tracker headquartered in Berlin that, per its own policy, handles health data under strict EU data-protection rules; its perimenopause mode adds symptom tracking and a cycle view accounting for changing cycle lengths. **Flo** offers, per the company, a Perimenopause Score it describes as validated specifically for perimenopause symptoms, mood and fatigue tracking, and an anonymous community.

**Best for:** Clue if privacy leads; Flo for assessment plus community. **Trade-offs:** cycle-first by origin.

## Making mood tracking actually work

Log at a consistent time daily rather than when you feel strongly — data collected only on bad days describes only bad days. Separate irritability from anxiety from low mood, since they respond to different things. Always log sleep alongside, because it is the most common driver. Note alcohol, which disrupts sleep architecture and stacks onto the next day. And review in multi-week blocks rather than daily, because the signal in this stage is the shape of a stretch.

## When mood needs a doctor, not an app

Raise it promptly with a healthcare professional if low mood is persistent, if you have lost interest in things you normally care about, if anxiety is interfering with daily life, if anger is escalating toward frightening you or someone else, or if you are having thoughts of harming yourself.

Depressive mood was reported by 77% of women in the Menopause Society survey — this is common ground, there are recognized management options for perimenopausal mood symptoms, and none of that is something a tracker can deliver.
