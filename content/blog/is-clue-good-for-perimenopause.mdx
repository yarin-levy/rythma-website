## What Clue offers for perimenopause

Clue Perimenopause is a mode within the app rather than a separate product. Per its makers, it adds symptom tracking relevant to this stage — hot flashes, sleep shifts, irregular cycles — and a cycle view designed to account for changing cycle lengths.

That second point deserves more credit than it usually gets. A tracker built on averaging recent cycles will, once perimenopause starts, spend most of the year telling you that you are weeks late. Redesigning the view so that changing lengths are the expected state rather than an anomaly is a small change with a large effect on whether the app feels like it is describing you. You can read Clue's own description in its [perimenopause overview](https://helloclue.com/articles/menopause/introducing-clue-perimenopause).

Alongside that sits Clue's general character: science-forward, restrained, notably less gamified than most of the category, with an emphasis on what data does and does not show.

## Where Clue genuinely helps in midlife

**Privacy is a real, not marketing, advantage.** Clue's Berlin base and EU data-protection posture are the strongest in the mainstream category. In a domain where reproductive health data is genuinely sensitive, that is a legitimate reason to choose an app — and a legitimate reason to stay with one.

**Its tone suits a stage full of hype.** Perimenopause attracts a great deal of confident advice. An app that declines to overstate what it knows is doing something valuable, particularly when the underlying biology genuinely is unpredictable. The Cleveland Clinic describes perimenopausal hormones as going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone rather than declining smoothly — no app can make that legible with certainty, and Clue does not pretend otherwise.

**It handles irregularity without breaking.** Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, and the late transition by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period. A cycle view built for changing lengths treats that as the normal case.

**Symptom logging reaches past bleeding.** Necessary, because perimenopause does. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue led at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all above hot flashes at 71%.

## Where Clue's cycle-first origin shows

**The cycle remains the organizing frame.** Even with the perimenopause mode, Clue is fundamentally a cycle tracker. In late perimenopause, where the U.S. Office on Women's Health notes you may skip months entirely and may not ovulate every cycle, the cycle becomes a progressively weaker container for your experience. Your symptoms keep happening; the frame around them gets thinner.

**No forecasting of difficult days.** Clue records and displays. It does not model your personal symptom history to tell you that the middle of next week is likely to be rough. That capability — prediction rather than retrospection — is what perimenopause-native, prediction-first apps like our own Rythma are built around, and it is the single biggest functional gap if planning is your goal.

**No community.** If part of what you want is other women describing the same thing, Clue does not offer it. Flo and Perry do.

**No structured doctor report.** You have your log; assembling a summary is your job. Balance offers a Health Report and Rythma generates a doctor report if that matters to you.

## Should you stay on Clue or switch?

**Stay if:** privacy is your leading concern; you value a calm, non-hyped tool; you are early in the transition and mainly want a reliable place to log; or you are on Android and want a trusted cross-platform tracker.

**Switch if:** you want your hard days forecast rather than recorded; you want a report built for handing to a clinician; you want peer community; or the cycle-centred framing has started to feel like it is describing someone else.

**What switching looks like.** The perimenopause-native apps differ by emphasis: Balance leads on clinician-backed education and a Health Report; Caria offers conversational AI guidance; Perry is built around community; Health & Her wraps a free tracker in a daily wellness toolkit; and Rythma — ours — is built around prediction and a doctor report. You are choosing which problem you want solved, not which app is objectively best.

## Getting more out of Clue if you stay

Log daily rather than in bursts, since gaps break any pattern you might otherwise notice. Track the symptoms that actually disrupt your days — sleep, energy, mood, brain fog — not only bleeding and flashes. Record intensity separately from occurrence. And review your own history monthly rather than only glancing at today, because in this stage the useful signal is the shape of several weeks, not any single entry.

Also know when to stop consulting an app. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause.
