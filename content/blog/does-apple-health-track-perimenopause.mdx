*Partly, and more usefully than most people assume. Apple's Cycle Tracking, built into the Health app on iPhone and available on Apple Watch, is free and does three things that matter in midlife. It logs periods, flow, spotting, and symptoms. Its Cycle Deviation Detection can notify you if your logged history over the previous six months shows a pattern of irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting — which is close to a description of perimenopause itself. And if a deviation is detected you can export the last 12 months of cycle history as a PDF to share with a healthcare provider. What it does not do is anything perimenopause-specific: no staging, no forecasting of which days will be difficult, and no interpretation of the wider symptom set that dominates this stage.*

Apple Health is the app almost every iPhone owner already has and almost nobody thinks of as a perimenopause tool. That is a fair instinct — it is not marketed as one — but the honest answer is more interesting than "no."

We make a perimenopause app ourselves (Rythma) and disclose that where relevant. Apple's features below are described from Apple's own support documentation.

## What Apple Cycle Tracking actually does

**Logging.** You can record periods, flow, spotting, and a range of symptoms in the Health app on iPhone or the Cycle Tracking app on Apple Watch. On supported Apple Watch models, wrist temperature sensing contributes to retrospective ovulation estimates.

**Predictions and notifications.** You can turn on period predictions and notifications for an approaching period, and fertile window estimates and notifications.

**Cycle Deviation Detection.** This is the part relevant to perimenopause. According to Apple, Cycle Tracking can inform you if your logged cycle history from the previous six months shows a pattern of **irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting**. You choose which deviations you want to be notified about.

**PDF export for your doctor.** If you receive a deviation alert, Apple states you can review your logged history and, if accurate, export the last 12 months of cycle history as a PDF to share with your healthcare provider.

You can read Apple's own description on its [Cycle Tracking support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356).

## Why the deviation detection matters more than it sounds

Read that list of deviations again — irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, persistent spotting — and compare it to how the transition is defined clinically.

Under the STRAW+10 staging system used by clinicians, the early menopause transition begins when consecutive cycle lengths persistently differ by seven or more days, and the late transition is marked by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period. The U.S. Office on Women's Health describes periods in perimenopause running longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, and skipping months entirely.

Apple is not staging perimenopause and does not claim to. But a free tool that watches six months of your history and tells you your cycles have become irregular or infrequent is, in practice, giving many women their first concrete signal that something has changed — often before they connect it to perimenopause at all.

The PDF export is genuinely valuable too. A dated 12-month record is exactly the evidence that turns "my periods have been weird" into a conversation a clinician can work with.

## Where Apple Health stops

**It is not perimenopause-specific.** There is no staging, no midlife framing, and no explanation of what irregular cycles mean at 47 versus at 27. You get the signal without the interpretation.

**It does not forecast difficult days.** This is the biggest gap. Apple can tell you a period may be approaching and that your cycles look irregular. It cannot tell you that next Tuesday and Wednesday are likely to be high-symptom days for you based on your own history — which is the thing that lets you plan.

**Its symptom coverage is not built around midlife.** And midlife is not mostly about bleeding. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue was the most reported symptom at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all outranking hot flashes at 71%. The NHS also lists brain fog, weight gain, palpitations, and joint aches. A cycle-centred logger captures a slice of that.

**Period predictions become less meaningful.** They rest on cycle-length regularity, which is exactly what the Cleveland Clinic's "rollercoaster" description of perimenopausal hormones undermines. Apple's own deviation detection is effectively telling you when to stop trusting the predictions.

## Who Apple Health is actually enough for

It is genuinely sufficient if you want a free, private, zero-effort record; you already live in the Apple ecosystem and will not open a second app; you are early in the transition and mainly want to notice that something is changing; or you specifically want a PDF to take to a doctor and nothing more.

It is not enough if you want to understand what is happening rather than just record it; you want difficult days forecast so you can plan around them; you want to track the fuller midlife symptom set and see what travels with what; or you want education, community, or guidance.

## Using it well if you stay

Turn on Cycle Deviation Detection explicitly — it is not automatic, and it is the most perimenopause-relevant thing the feature offers. Log spotting as spotting rather than as a period, since the deviation logic depends on the distinction. Log consistently, because six months of patchy data produces nothing. And export the PDF before an appointment rather than trying to recall a year of cycles in the room.

Know when to stop consulting any app, too. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause.
