*Apple Health's Cycle Tracking is free, built into every iPhone, and better for perimenopause than its reputation suggests: it logs periods and symptoms, and its Cycle Deviation Detection can notify you when six months of history show irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting — then export 12 months as a PDF for your doctor. What it does not do is interpret any of it for midlife. Rythma is a paid-tier app built for perimenopause specifically: it learns your personal symptom patterns, forecasts difficult days before they arrive, and generates a doctor report. If you want a free record and a nudge when your cycles change, Apple Health is genuinely enough. If you want to plan your life around symptoms you can see coming, that is a different tool.*

This is the honest version of a comparison that is usually stacked. Apple Health is free and already on your phone, which means any paid app has to justify itself rather than assume it wins. Rythma is our app, and we will be specific about where free is sufficient.

## What each one is

**Apple Cycle Tracking** is a feature of the Health app on iPhone, with a companion app on Apple Watch. It logs periods, flow, spotting, and symptoms; offers period predictions and notifications; and, on supported Watch models, uses wrist temperature sensing for retrospective ovulation estimates. Per Apple, its Cycle Deviation Detection can notify you if your logged history from the previous six months shows a pattern of irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, or persistent spotting, and lets you export the last 12 months of cycle history as a PDF to share with a healthcare provider. See Apple's [Cycle Tracking support page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356).

**Rythma** is a standalone iPhone app built for perimenopause from the start. It expects irregularity rather than assuming a monthly rhythm, learns your individual symptom patterns as you log, forecasts high-symptom stretches before they arrive, and generates a shareable doctor report. See the [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rythma-perimenopause-tracker/id6762185611).

## Where Apple Health is genuinely good

**It costs nothing and you already have it.** That is not a small thing. The best tracker is the one you actually open, and an app already on your home screen has an enormous advantage over one you have to remember to download.

**Its deviation detection maps closely onto perimenopause.** Irregular cycles, infrequent periods, prolonged periods, persistent spotting — that list is close to a plain-language description of the transition. Under the STRAW+10 staging system, the early menopause transition begins when consecutive cycle lengths persistently differ by seven or more days, and the late transition is marked by a gap of 60 or more days. A free feature that watches six months and flags exactly that pattern is doing real work.

**The PDF export is a legitimately good doctor-visit tool.** Twelve dated months beats memory every time.

**Privacy.** Data stays in Apple's health framework rather than a third-party product, which some women weigh heavily for reproductive health data.

## Where Apple Health stops

**Signal without interpretation.** Apple tells you your cycles look irregular. It does not tell you what irregular cycles mean at 47, what stage that suggests, or what else to expect. You are handed a fact and left to research it.

**No forecasting of hard days.** This is the functional core of the difference. Apple predicts periods — a prediction that becomes less meaningful precisely as perimenopause progresses, since the Cleveland Clinic describes hormones fluctuating "like a rollercoaster" rather than declining smoothly. It does not predict symptom load.

**Cycle-centred symptom coverage.** Perimenopause is mostly not about bleeding. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue led at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all above hot flashes at 71%.

## Where Rythma adds something

**It forecasts rather than records.** Rythma's purpose is telling you which upcoming days are likely to be difficult, based on your own logged history — so you can move a demanding meeting or protect a weekend rather than discovering on the day.

**It is built around midlife symptoms, not the cycle.** Fatigue, sleep, mood, brain fog, and hot flashes are the primary objects, with bleeding as one input among several. In late perimenopause, where the U.S. Office on Women's Health notes you may skip months entirely and may not ovulate every cycle, that reframing matters.

**Its doctor report is symptom-led.** Apple's PDF is a cycle history. Rythma's report summarizes symptoms and trends, which is closer to what a midlife appointment is actually about.

**Trade-offs, honestly.** Rythma is iPhone-only, prediction improves the longer you log so early weeks are mostly data-gathering, and unlike Apple Health it is a separate app you have to remember to open. Apple Health also collects sleep and activity passively in the background, which Rythma does not replicate.

## Which should you use?

- **You want a free record and a nudge when things change.** Apple Health is enough. Turn on Cycle Deviation Detection.
- **You want to know which days will be hard so you can plan.** That is Rythma's job.
- **You want a doctor-visit document.** Both offer one; Apple's is cycle history, Rythma's is symptom trends.
- **You are on Android.** Neither applies — look at Clue, Flo, Balance, Caria, or Perry.
- **You will not open a second app.** Be honest about this. Apple Health used consistently beats a better app used twice.

Using both is also reasonable: Apple Health passively collecting sleep and activity, a perimenopause app doing the interpretation.

## When to stop consulting an app entirely

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding — soaking a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours in a row — as well as bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause. Tracking is what makes those patterns easy to spot and describe; it is not what resolves them.
