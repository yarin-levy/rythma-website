*For education and appointment preparation, yes. Balance, from Newson Health and founded by Dr. Louise Newson, offers symptom tracking across midlife complaints, a large library of evidence-based articles, and a Health Report you can take to appointments; per its makers it has been recognized by the NHS and certified by the digital-health assessor ORCHA. That combination — clinical authorship plus a document you can hand over — is genuinely useful in a stage where symptoms get attributed to stress. Where it fits less well: its focus is broad menopause rather than perimenopause specifically, it records and explains rather than forecasting which of your days will be difficult, and some features sit behind a paid tier. If your main need is understanding and being taken seriously, it earns its place. If it is anticipating hard days, that is a different tool.*

"Worth it" depends entirely on what you want it for, so this is organized around that rather than around a verdict. We make a perimenopause app ourselves — Rythma — and say so where relevant. Balance is described from its own published materials.

## What Balance actually offers

**Symptom tracking** across midlife complaints, covering the wide range this stage involves rather than bleeding alone.

**A large library of evidence-based articles**, which is the app's centre of gravity rather than an add-on.

**A Health Report** you can take to appointments, summarizing what you have logged.

**Credibility markers**: founded by Dr. Louise Newson through Newson Health, and per its makers recognized by the NHS and certified by ORCHA.

You can confirm current features and pricing on the [Balance app page](https://www.balance-menopause.com/balance-app/).

## Where Balance is genuinely worth it

**When you need to be taken seriously.** This is the strongest case for it. A very common experience in this stage is having symptoms attributed to stress or age for years before anyone mentions perimenopause. Arriving with a Health Report from a clinician-founded, NHS-recognized product, plus the reading to explain what you are asking about, is a materially different position than arriving with a recollection.

**When the problem is not knowing what is happening.** The symptom set is far wider than most people expect. The NHS lists hot flushes and night sweats, sleep problems, mood changes, brain fog, weight gain, palpitations, and joint aches. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue led at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%) — all above hot flashes at 71%. Discovering your scattered complaints are a recognized cluster is itself a relief, and Balance is built to deliver that.

**When you want provenance.** Perimenopause information online is saturated with supplement marketing and confident nonsense. Content with named clinical authorship is worth more than a chat interface's fluent answer, particularly if you intend to raise it with a doctor.

**When you are considering treatment options.** Understanding what exists, and the evidence behind it, before an appointment makes for a much better appointment. Balance is strong here.

## Where it fits less well

**It is a menopause app, not a perimenopause app.** The distinction matters. Menopause is a single point in time — 12 consecutive months since your final period with no other medical cause, per the World Health Organization. Perimenopause is the years of instability before it, which is a different problem with a different texture. Balance covers the whole territory, which means it is less specifically tuned to the unpredictability that defines the earlier stage.

**It records and explains; it does not forecast.** This is the biggest functional gap. Balance can tell you why you feel terrible and give you a document about it. It is not designed to learn your personal symptom pattern and tell you that next Tuesday and Wednesday look rough, which is the capability that lets you actually plan. That is what prediction-first apps such as our own Rythma are built around.

**Some features are behind a paid tier.** Check the current pricing rather than assuming what is included.

**Reading is slower than asking.** If your instinct is to ask a question and get an answer rather than find and read the relevant article, Caria's conversational AI guidance may suit you better — with the caveat that AI answers are information rather than medical advice and lack citable authorship.

**It is not a community.** If isolation is your main problem, Perry is built around peer support and Flo has an anonymous in-app community.

## Is it worth paying for?

A reasonable way to decide: the free tier is enough if you mainly want the articles and basic logging. Paying makes more sense if you will genuinely use the tracking and report — meaning you will log consistently for at least eight weeks before an appointment, since a report built on a fortnight of scattered entries documents very little.

Be honest with yourself about that. The most common way any of these apps fails is not a missing feature; it is being opened twice and abandoned.

## Who should choose something else

- **"I want to know which days will be hard."** A prediction-first perimenopause app.
- **"I want an answer now, not an article."** Caria.
- **"I feel alone in this."** Perry, or Flo's community.
- **"Privacy is my priority."** Clue, headquartered in Berlin and handling health data under strict EU data-protection rules per its own policy.
- **"I want free and minimal."** Apple Health logs the basics and can export 12 months of cycle history as a PDF for your doctor.

## Getting the most from it

Log consistently rather than when symptoms spike, since data gathered only on bad days describes only bad days. Track intensity, not just occurrence. Keep logging bleeding even when it becomes erratic — that is the clinically interesting part, and it is how you catch what the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says warrants attention: very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon hourly for two or more hours), bleeding between periods or after sex, cycles staying consistently closer than about 21 days, or any bleeding after menopause. And read before your appointment, not after, so you arrive knowing what you want to ask.
