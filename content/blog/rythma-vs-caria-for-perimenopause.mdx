## Why perimenopause needs a midlife-first app

Perimenopause is the transition leading up to your final period. According to the U.S. Office on Women's Health, it usually begins in your mid- to late 40s and lasts about four years on average, though it can run as long as eight. Its defining feature is unpredictability: periods may run longer or shorter, heavier or lighter, skip months entirely, and you may not ovulate every cycle.

The hormone picture is not a smooth decline either. The Cleveland Clinic describes perimenopausal hormone levels as going up and down "like a rollercoaster," with estrogen swinging out of balance with progesterone rather than tapering off predictably.

And the symptoms reach well past bleeding. In an international survey of more than 17,000 women across 158 countries analyzed by The Menopause Society, fatigue was the most reported symptom at 83%, ahead of irritability (80%), depressive mood (77%), and sleep problems (76%). Hot flashes — the symptom most associated with this stage — were recognized as a perimenopause sign by only 71%.

Both Caria and Rythma were designed with that reality in mind. Neither is fighting the 28-day assumption baked into standard period apps. The question is which of them fits how you want to be helped.

## Caria — AI-assisted guidance for midlife

Caria is a perimenopause and menopause companion app built for this stage rather than adapted from a cycle tracker. Alongside symptom logging, its distinguishing feature is conversational AI guidance: you can ask a question in plain language and get information framed for perimenopause and menopause.

That matters more than a feature list suggests. A large part of this transition is uncertainty — "is this normal, is this even perimenopause, what should I ask my doctor." Having somewhere to bring that question at 2am, without waiting for an appointment or a community reply, is a real convenience. For someone whose main frustration is not knowing what is happening to them, an on-demand explainer is a sensible answer.

Where Caria stops is forecasting. It is built to respond to what you are experiencing, not to tell you in advance which days next week are likely to be rough. AI guidance is also not medical advice, and its outputs should be treated as information rather than diagnosis.

**Best for:** women who want conversational, always-available guidance alongside symptom tracking, especially early in the transition when the main need is understanding.

Rythma is our app, so we will be specific rather than complimentary. It is an iPhone app built for perimenopause from the start. Instead of assuming a fixed monthly cycle, it expects irregularity and learns your individual symptom patterns over time.

## Head-to-head: the honest differences

**Explanation vs. anticipation.** This is the core split. Caria is strongest when the question is "what is this?" Rythma is strongest when the question is "what is coming?" Both are legitimate needs, and which one dominates usually depends on how far into the transition you are. Early on, explanation tends to matter more. Once you have accepted the diagnosis and the problem becomes managing your life around it, anticipation takes over.

**The doctor visit.** Both apps give you a symptom history to draw on. Rythma additionally generates a report designed to be handed over. Either way, consistent logging is what lets you catch the bleeding changes the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says warrant medical attention — very heavy bleeding (soaking a pad or tampon hourly for two or more hours), bleeding between periods or after sex, or cycles that stay consistently closer together than about 21 days.

**How each handles irregularity.** Neither app depends on a regular cycle, which is the main reason both outperform a standard tracker here. Under the STRAW+10 staging system used by clinicians, the early menopause transition is defined by consecutive cycle lengths persistently differing by seven or more days, and the late transition by a stretch of 60 or more days without a period. An app that treats that as normal input rather than an error is doing its job.

**Platform.** Rythma is iPhone-only today. Check Caria's current platform availability on its store listing if you are on Android — that alone may settle it.

- **You mainly want to understand what is happening to you.** Caria's conversational guidance is built for that.
- **You mainly want to know which days will be hard, and to plan around them.** Rythma is built for that.
- **You want a structured summary to hand your doctor.** Rythma generates one; otherwise you are assembling it yourself.
- **You are on Android.** Rythma is iPhone-only today, so check availability first.

You do not have to pick permanently. Both are free to download, and the honest answer is that many women use a tracker for a few weeks before deciding what they actually need from it. Whatever you choose, the value comes from consistent logging — patterns and predictions only emerge once there is enough data behind them.
